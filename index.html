01 · The sending

A community sends them out

Acts 13:1–3

The journey begins not with a lone adventurer, but with a worshipping community listening together.

At Antioch in Syria, prophets and teachers include Barnabas and Saul (later known by his Roman name, Paul). While the community is worshipping and fasting, the Holy Spirit calls Barnabas and Saul to a work. After prayer and the laying on of hands, the community sends them on their way.

They travel first to Seleucia, Antioch’s port, and sail for Cyprus. John Mark goes with them as their assistant. The account doesn’t give a ship’s name, travel time, or exact route at sea; the map shows the broad direction, not a modern navigational chart.

Keep the names straight: Saul is also called Paul from Acts 13:9 onward. This is the first of the journeys traditionally called Paul’s missionary journeys.

02 · Across to Cyprus

First, the island’s eastern port

Acts 13:4–5

From Seleucia, the team crosses the eastern Mediterranean to Cyprus, Barnabas’s home region.

They land at Salamis, on Cyprus’s east coast, and proclaim the word of God in the Jewish synagogues. John Mark is with them. Luke gives only a brief description here, but the choice of Salamis puts the travelers in a major town with an established Jewish community.

Cyprus is not just a stop on the way somewhere else: the team travels across the island before leaving it. The narrative names the ports and people that matter to its account, rather than cataloguing every village or mile.

On the map: The dotted sea route is illustrative. Acts says they sailed from Seleucia to Cyprus; it does not specify their exact course.

03 · The governor listens

A meeting in Paphos

Acts 13:6–12

On the western side of Cyprus, the message reaches the Roman governor’s court.

In Paphos, the missionaries meet Sergius Paulus, the proconsul, who wants to hear God’s word. Elymas, a Jewish magician and false prophet, opposes them and tries to turn the proconsul away from the faith. Paul confronts him; Luke describes Elymas temporarily losing his sight.

Sergius Paulus believes, amazed at the teaching about the Lord. Acts presents this as a decisive encounter, not a formula for how every conversation or conversion will unfold.

Names in Acts: The text calls the proconsul “an intelligent man” and distinguishes him from Elymas. It does not say that the whole island converted.

04 · A change in the team

From the coast, inland

Acts 13:13

The travelers leave Cyprus for Perga, in Pamphylia on the southern coast of Asia Minor.

At Perga, John Mark leaves the group and returns to Jerusalem. Luke reports the departure without explaining his reason here. The remaining team—Paul and Barnabas—continues inland toward Pisidian Antioch.

That change matters later: Acts 15 records a sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas about taking John Mark on another journey. For now, the story moves north from the coast into the uplands.

Geography: “Pisidian Antioch” is a different city from Antioch in Syria, where the journey began. The qualifier “of Pisidia” helps distinguish them.

05 · A sermon and a split response

Good news in the synagogue

Acts 13:14–52

Invited to speak, Paul traces Israel’s story and announces Jesus as its fulfillment.

On a Sabbath in Pisidian Antioch, Paul speaks in the synagogue. He recalls God’s care for Israel, the kings, and David, then proclaims Jesus’ death and resurrection and forgiveness through him. The speech is one of Acts’ fullest examples of Paul addressing a synagogue audience.

Many listeners ask to hear more. The next Sabbath, a large crowd gathers; some welcome the message, while others oppose Paul and Barnabas. The missionaries say they will also turn to the Gentiles. Acts describes both joy among many new believers and the pair’s expulsion from the district.

Not a simple “everyone versus everyone” story: Luke describes varied responses among both Jewish and Gentile hearers. Paul and Barnabas shake the dust from their feet and continue, while new disciples remain.

06 · Staying despite opposition

In Iconium, the work grows

Acts 14:1–7

In Iconium, Paul and Barnabas again begin in the synagogue—and again the response is divided.

Many Jews and Greeks believe. Others stir up opposition. The two remain for a considerable time, speaking boldly, and Acts says signs and wonders accompany their message. The city becomes divided; when a plan to mistreat and stone them comes to light, they flee to the surrounding region.

They do not interpret leaving as the end of the mission. They go on to nearby towns and keep proclaiming the good news.

A repeated pattern: proclamation, a mixed response, the formation of a community, and movement onward. The route is not frictionless, but neither is it simply a story of retreat.

07 · Misunderstood in Lystra

When the crowd gets it wrong

Acts 14:8–20

A healing draws a crowd, but the crowd misunderstands who the missionaries are.

In Lystra, Paul heals a man who had been unable to walk since birth. The people cry out in the local Lycaonian language, calling Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes. The priest of Zeus brings oxen and garlands, preparing a sacrifice.

Paul and Barnabas tear their clothes and rush into the crowd. They insist they are human beings and point instead to the living God, creator of heaven, earth, sea, and all that is in them. Soon, opponents arrive from Pisidian Antioch and Iconium; Paul is stoned and dragged outside the city, but survives.

Acts 14:11 notes that the crowd spoke in Lycaonian. The text’s focus is the sharp contrast between the crowd’s acclaim and the missionaries’ insistence that worship belongs to God.

08 · Further along the road

Derbe—and then back again

Acts 14:20–23

With the disciples around him, Paul gets up and goes with Barnabas to Derbe.

They proclaim the good news in Derbe and make many disciples. Then comes a striking choice: instead of taking the easiest route home, they retrace their steps through Lystra, Iconium, and Pisidian Antioch—the places where they had faced danger.

They strengthen the disciples, encourage them to remain in the faith, and appoint elders in each church with prayer and fasting. The outward journey has become a return journey of care: the new communities are not left behind.

Map key: The terracotta dotted line marks the outward route; the green dotted line marks the return through the inland towns. The map is schematic and not to scale.

09 · Completing the circuit

Back through Pamphylia

Acts 14:24–26

After traveling through Pisidia, Paul and Barnabas reach Pamphylia and speak in Perga.

They then go down to Attalia, a port on the coast, and sail back to Antioch in Syria—the community that had entrusted them to God’s grace for this work. Acts gives no sailing details or precise dates for these stages.

Their return closes a geographic loop, but it also brings the story home to the people who sent them. The journey is not a solo achievement: it is shared, supported, and reported back.

Two Antiochs: Pisidian Antioch lies inland in Asia Minor; Syrian Antioch is the eastern Mediterranean base where the journey began and ended.

10 · The report

They gather the church

Acts 14:27–28

Back in Antioch, they gather the community and tell what God has done with them.

They report that God has opened a door of faith for the Gentiles, and they stay with the disciples for a long time. The account ends not with a triumphant travelogue, but with a shared testimony and a growing community.

Acts 13–14 records places, speeches, welcome, conflict, healing, danger, and return. Its route can be followed on a map; its deeper arc is the formation and strengthening of communities across cultural and geographic boundaries.
